This thesis builds two complementary pieces: a set of stock-return prediction models
designed to assess the potential of Stockformer, and a portfolio optimisation system with
explicit transaction-cost control. Stockformer, the model under evaluation, is a network
of roughly one million parameters that combines a wavelet decomposition, attention and a
graph of stock relations; it was calibrated on Chinese A-share stocks and is retrained
here on the S\&P 500, a market structurally different from the one it was designed for.
The evaluation addresses two chained questions: whether the model provides predictive
power in that market and, if it does not, where the net return observed there comes from.

The first part, which carries the main weight of the thesis
(Chapter~\ref{cap:parte1}), assesses the potential of Stockformer by comparing it with
simpler models (regularised regressions and boosted trees) on a common data panel, under
a common evaluation framework and at a daily horizon. The main metric is the rank
information coefficient (IC), which measures the correlation between the predicted and
the realised ordering of returns. Possible information leakage is also audited; the only
incident found favours the complex model and is reported separately. The comparison comes
out against Stockformer: its out-of-sample IC is $-0.003$, indistinguishable from zero,
while a five-coefficient Lasso regression reaches $+0.0238$, and all the simple models
come out ahead, although by small margins. On that evidence the model is rejected for the
universe analysed (477 S\&P 500 constituents with complete price coverage): its
complexity does not translate into predictive power that would justify adopting it over
much simpler alternatives.

The second part puts the portfolio optimisation system to work and studies the origin of
the net return of a weekly market-neutral strategy on the same universe. A stage-by-stage attribution shows that the signal produces a weak
gross return and that portfolio construction with explicit transaction-cost control is the
stage that preserves it in net terms. A signal search with criteria fixed in advance, and
validated on a reserved sample, points to residual momentum as the most promising
candidate. Combined with that construction, the net Sharpe ratio rises from $0.38$ to
$0.72$ over a 311-week window and keeps its sign on the reserved sample. The improvement
is presented as the most promising result of the thesis, pending confirmation with more
data.

The conclusions are conditional on the study design: a universe restricted to current
index constituents with complete coverage, public data without historical reconstruction,
a single run of the model and a loss function adapted from the original. Under those
conditions, the contribution is twofold: empirical evidence of absence of transfer in this
case, and a reproducible evaluation and attribution procedure that places the origin of
the net return in a simple signal and in portfolio construction, not in the complexity of
the predictor.
